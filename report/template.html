Olist Marketplace · Analysis Report · Sep 2016 – Oct 2018

The Promise Problem

Olist promises delivery in a median of 23 days and delivers in 10. That twelve-day padding looks like broken forecasting. It is the most valuable thing the company owns — and it is being spent.

Orders analysed 99,441 Sellers 3,095 Customers 96,096 Reviews 99,441
01 — Problem understanding

What leadership asked, and what the data can actually answer

Olist connects small Brazilian merchants to the major marketplaces through a single contract. When a customer buys, the responsible seller is notified, ships through Olist's logistics partners, and — once the order arrives, or once the estimated delivery date passes — the customer receives a satisfaction survey.

That last clause turns out to matter enormously, and we return to it in section 4.

Leadership has two years of orders, payments, reviews and delivery timestamps, and no consolidated view connecting them. The question is what is actually shaping customer experience, and where the clearest opportunities are.

One framing decision shapes everything below. Review scores are bimodal — 57% are five stars, 12% are one star, and there is very little in between. A mean over that distribution averages two populations that barely overlap. So where it matters we model P(1-star): the business question is not "how satisfied is the average customer" but "what makes someone furious".

02 — Analytical approach

Structured as an investigation, not a survey

Every table was reduced to a single analytical row per order — 99,441 rows, 65 columns — with seven cleaning decisions made once and documented, including two that materially change the answers: 775 orders have no line items at all (overwhelmingly the cancelled and unavailable ones, so an inner join silently deletes the platform's worst outcomes), and reviews contain 827 duplicate identifiers across 555 orders surveyed more than once.

Time series are restricted to January 2017 – August 2018. The months outside that window hold 4, 1, 16 and 4 orders respectively — export artifacts that would otherwise invent a collapse at both ends of every chart.

Beyond description, three things carry the argument: a regression discontinuity that tests our own headline hypothesis and rejects it, a logistic driver model that ranks causes of one-star reviews by effect size, and a held-out predictive model that turns the central finding into an operating policy.

03 — Key insight

Satisfaction is a cliff, not a slope

This is the finding everything else hangs from. Customer satisfaction does not decay gradually as delivery slips. It holds almost perfectly flat right up to the promised date, then collapses.

The strategic consequence is immediate. Olist's twelve-day buffer is not sloppy forecasting — it is a correctly-priced insurance policy against a wildly asymmetric downside. 04 — The hypothesis we killed

A smoking gun that turned out to be nothing

Olist's survey fires at estimated_delivery_date + 2 days — median exactly 2.0, inter-quartile range 2 to 2. So when an order runs late, the customer is asked to rate it before it arrives.

37% of every 1-star review in this dataset was written by someone who did not yet have their product.

That looked like a measurement artifact depressing Olist's scores, with a cheap fix: delay the survey. Before recommending it, we tested it.

The trigger rule creates a sharp discontinuity — an order is surveyed pre-delivery if and only if it runs more than two days late. A customer 1.9 days late and one 2.1 days late had near-identical experiences but received a different survey. Any jump in score exactly at that threshold is the survey effect, net of lateness.

There is no jump. The estimate is +0.15 stars (SE 0.19), indistinguishable from zero at every bandwidth tested; placebo cutoffs show two days is unremarkable; a donut specification agrees. The 95% confidence interval rules out anything above 0.52 stars against a raw gap of 1.50. Roughly 1.35 of those 1.50 stars is simply the order being late.

The hypothesis is dead, and killing it was worth more than keeping it. It validates the review score as a genuine measure rather than an artifact, and it eliminates a plausible-sounding fix that would have done nothing.

But the text tells a second story. The scores are identical; the content is not.

The recommendation changes accordingly: not delay the survey, but act on it.

05 — Business findings

Where the failures actually live

Decomposing every late order into its three observable stages settles a question that otherwise invites guessing. Of the roughly nineteen extra median days on a late delivery, seventeen are carrier transit and 1.3 is seller handoff. Payment approval is noise.

That points the geography analysis at distance rather than administration — and the map makes it obvious.

One state breaks the rule

Distance explains almost everything — and completely fails on Rio de Janeiro, which sits closer to its sellers than Paraná (394 km against 426) yet runs a 13.5% late rate against Paraná's 4.9% on the platform's second-largest market.

Decomposing the 1.62-day gap: 72% is carrier transit, 3% is seller handoff. Orders into Rio run 13.5% late; orders shipped out of Rio by Rio sellers run 9.1%. Rio's sellers are fine — it is a destination-side last-mile problem, and it is worst in the periphery, not the capital: São Gonçalo 24.9% late, Macaé 18.4%, against Rio city's 11.8%.

The worst two months were not a demand shock

February–March 2018 are the worst months in the dataset — 21.4% late, 3.74 stars — and they are three months after Black Friday. Order volume rose only 7.8%. Carrier transit rose 1.89 days while seller handoff held flat, all twelve measurable states worsened, and 86% of sellers degraded simultaneously.

When nearly every seller in the country gets worse at once while their own handoff times hold steady, it is a shared dependency failing. Both anomalies land on the same culprit.

There is a positive story attached: from April 2018 transit fell 6.9 → 6.5 → 5.9 → 5.3 → 4.9 days. Olist fixed it and kept going. The lever works; they have pulled it before.

06 — Root cause

What actually drives a one-star review

A logistic model of P(1-star) across 95,978 delivered orders, with continuous terms standardised so effects are comparable. It separates three things the raw data confounds: breaking the promise, the absolute wait regardless of promise, and everything else.

The largest single driver was not on our list

An order split across multiple sellers is five times more likely to receive a one-star review, controlling for everything else including lateness. An odds ratio that large is suspicious, so we tried to break it four ways.

  • Controlling for lateness: among on-time orders, multi-seller runs 36.0% one-star against 6.4% — a 5.6× gap.
  • Same basket, different split: holding item count fixed at 2, 3, 4 and 5 items, multi-seller loses ~0.9 stars every time. It is the seller count, not the item count.
  • The text confirms the mechanism: "only part arrived" appears in 29.6% of multi-seller one-stars against 7.5% otherwise.
  • The smoking gun: multi-seller orders are recorded as delivered faster (7.9 vs 10.3 days) and late less often. That is backwards — unless the timestamp is capturing the first parcel rather than the last.

Olist stores one delivery date per order. A multi-seller order ships as several parcels — so it is marked complete, and surveyed, when the first one lands.

The customer is asked to rate a finished order while holding half of one.

The honest accounting

It would be convenient to conclude that delivery explains everything. It does not.

48.8% of one-star reviews are a delivery failure of one kind or another. The other 51.2% arrived on time and the customer was unhappy anyway. The promise is the biggest single lever available. It is not the whole story, and a report that claimed otherwise would be overselling.

Two things sit inside that residual. The multi-seller completion bug above is one. The other is genuine product and expectation mismatch — office_furniture scores 3.75 even when delivered on time, more than half a star below the platform's on-time average, while books reaches 4.64. We tested whether listing quality explained it (photo count, description length — the fields the data dictionary flags as listing-quality proxies) and found the effect real but negligible: under 0.1 stars across the full range.

The remaining 17.7% deserves separate ownership. 2,963 orders never arrived at all and carry a 70.5% one-star rate. That is an inventory-accuracy problem, and no amount of logistics improvement will reach it.

07 — From finding to policy

Promise per order, not per platform

The analysis says protect the promise and do not cut the buffer. But Olist's buffer is flat — identical padding for a 160 km São Paulo order and a 2,400 km order to Pará. That is the worst of both worlds: too slow where it is safe, too tight where it is not.

So rather than one promise for the platform, predict each order's delivery-time distribution at checkout and promise a chosen quantile of it. Trained on 71,118 orders through April 2018, tested on 25,352 unseen orders from May–August. Every feature is knowable at checkout; seller history is built from the training window only.

And a scorecard that targets the right sellers

A raw seller rating is unfair and unactionable in both directions: a seller shipping heavy furniture to Pará is set up to score badly, and a 2.1-star seller with nine orders is a rounding error beside a 3.9-star seller with nine hundred. So each seller is benchmarked against their own mix — the platform average for the same categories at the same distances — then ranked by stars lost, which is volume × shortfall.

The spread survives the control: 0.75 stars wide from the 10th to the 90th percentile after adjusting for what sellers sell and how far they ship. And the mechanism is one they control — mix-adjusted performance correlates −0.37 with handoff time, and the worst decile hands off in 3.0 days against the best decile's 1.1.

Two findings make this immediately actionable: seller size is entirely uncorrelated with quality (r = −0.011), and the top 100 sellers control 55% of revenue while performing only averagely. A hundred conversations reach half the platform.

08 — The number nobody is looking at

Only 3.1% of customers ever come back

Across 96,096 people and 99,441 orders, just 2,997 customers ever placed a second order. Restricting to a cohort with at least 180 days of observable follow-up, the return rate is 3.79% — and a one- or two-star first order returns at 3.43% against 4.06% for a five-star one, a 15.6% relative gap.

That differential is directionally real, but the base rate is the finding. Olist re-acquires almost its entire customer base every year. Repeat buyers spend 1.89× more over their lifetime and are 3.1% of customers driving 5.7% of revenue. Satisfaction moves retention, but nowhere near enough to save it on its own.

Everything above is about defending a transaction. This number asks whether there is a business being built on top of them, and the data says: not yet.

09 — Recommendations

What Olist should do

  1. Promise per order, not per platform

    Replace the flat buffer with a predicted delivery-time quantile per order. Demonstrated on 25,352 held-out orders: 47% fewer late deliveries at an identical mean promise length, or the same lateness with promises 1.3–2.9 days shorter.

  2. Do not mark an order delivered — or fire the survey — until the last parcel lands

    The single largest driver of one-star reviews is a data-model artifact. Multi-seller orders run 36% one-star even when on time. A notification and timestamp change, not a capital project.

  3. Route pre-delivery one-star reviews into live recovery

    8,446 customers report non-receipt while their order is still in flight. Treat those as support tickets, not sentiment. Note the regression discontinuity says this will not raise the score — the value is recovering the customer, not the metric.

  4. Set a seller SLA on handoff time, targeted by stars lost

    Handoff is the part of the delay sellers control, and it separates the best decile from the worst by nearly two days. Start with the twenty sellers carrying 39% of all stars lost — ranked by impact, not by rating.

  5. Give non-delivery its own owner

    2,963 orders never arrive and carry a 70.5% one-star rate — 2,089 one-star reviews that no delivery improvement can reach. This is inventory accuracy and seller-listing hygiene, and it is being managed as though it were logistics.

  6. Attack the carrier leg, starting with Rio's last mile

    87% of excess delay is transit, not sellers. Rio is the nearest-term, highest-density target: a 13.5% late rate at short distance on the second-biggest market, concentrated in the periphery. Regional fulfilment capacity for the North and Northeast is the structural version of the same fix.

Three recommendations this analysis killed

  1. "Your delivery estimates are inaccurate — tighten them." The asymmetry says earliness buys 0.10 stars and lateness costs 1.45. And Olist already ran this experiment: the promise fell 39 days to 13 while the late rate tripled.
  2. "Delay the survey so customers aren't rating undelivered orders." Intuitive, and wrong. The regression discontinuity at the two-day trigger returns +0.15 stars (SE 0.19). The anger is real; moving the survey moves nothing.
  3. "Focus on your worst-rated sellers." Raw rating targets small sellers with noisy averages. Benchmarking against each seller's own category and distance mix, then ranking by stars lost, targets the ones whose underperformance actually reaches customers.
10 — Limitations

What this analysis cannot tell you

This is observational data. We identify associations; only the regression discontinuity in section 4 supports a causal reading, and it is a null result. The driver model ranks factors by conditional association, not by proven effect.

Reviews arrive for 99.4% of orders, so selection into reviewing is not a serious concern — but the survey trigger means late orders are systematically rated earlier in their lifecycle than on-time ones, which we quantify rather than correct.

The dataset ends abruptly in August 2018, so the encouraging trend in the final months cannot be confirmed as durable. Distances are great-circle between zip-prefix centroids, not routed road distance, and roughly 1.3% of orders cannot be located at all. Seller and customer identifiers are hashed, so no individual seller can be verified outside the data.

Finally, the risk model is trained and tested inside a single platform's operating regime. It predicts what delivery times were under Olist's existing carrier mix; it does not forecast what they would become if that mix changed.

Method and reproducibility. All figures and statistics regenerate from the accompanying notebook, which runs top to bottom without manual steps. Statistical routines — OLS with HC1 robust errors, the regression discontinuity, and the logistic driver model by IRLS — are implemented in numpy rather than statsmodels, whose 0.14.4 release fails to import against scipy ≥ 1.16.
